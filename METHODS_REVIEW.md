# Methods review: Katz and Shapiro (1985)

- **Paper.** Michael L. Katz and Carl Shapiro, "Network Externalities, Competition, and Compatibility," *American
  Economic Review* 75(3), June 1985, pp. 424–440.
- **Statistical focus.** The paper's two formal claims that later work relies on most: that industry output is
  greater under compatibility than in any equilibrium with some incompatible firms, and that firms with large
  networks or good reputations are biased against compatibility while firms with small networks or weak
  reputations are biased toward it.
- **Review standard.** Internal consistency of the formal results, explicit assumptions, and the testability of
  predictions, as for any theory paper. The AEA Data and Code Availability Policy does not apply, since the paper
  uses no data.
- **What was read.** I did not read the full text of the 1985 article. The only free copy I located was on a
  third-party site that was unreachable and whose authorization I could not confirm, and the JSTOR version is behind
  a subscription. I read the article's abstract (Consortium for Standards metalibrary entry); the authors' own later
  survey, Katz and Shapiro, "Systems Competition and Network Effects," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 8(2), 1994,
  pp. 93–115, in full from Carl Shapiro's Berkeley page; Nicholas Economides, "The Economics of Networks,"
  *International Journal of Industrial Organization* 14 (1996), pp. 673–699, in full, which summarizes the 1985
  results on pp. 680–685; Matthew Nagler, "Network Externalities, Mutuality, and Compatibility," NET Institute
  Working Paper 08-37 (2008), in full, which restates the size-and-compatibility result; and Mark Jamison's working
  paper on network externalities and mergers (University of Florida PURC), which describes the 1985 demand setup.
  Everything below about the paper's content comes from those secondary accounts and is marked accordingly. Page
  and equation numbers inside the 1985 article could not be checked.

## Methods summary

The paper is a static equilibrium model of oligopoly with consumption externalities. As described by Economides
(1996, pp. 680–681), each buyer's willingness to pay for a unit depends on the quantity of the good expected to be
sold, rising with that expectation, and in equilibrium expectations are fulfilled. The 1994 survey (pp. 94–95)
lists the 1985 paper as the source for treating hardware and software as a "virtual network" that behaves like a
physical one. Firms compete in quantities. Compatibility is modeled as a choice that pools networks, achieved
either by joint standardization or by an adapter that one firm can build alone (Nagler 2008, describing the
"adaptor" technology "in Katz and Shapiro's (1985) parlance"). Results compare equilibria under compatibility and
incompatibility, ask which firms gain from moving between them, and compare those private gains with changes in
total surplus.

**Research design.** Formal theory, with comparative statics across compatibility regimes. No empirical design.

**Sampling strategy.** Does not apply. There is no sample.

**Sample size.** Does not apply.

**Inclusion and exclusion criteria.** Does not apply to data. The model's scope conditions play this role. Goods
must be bought in single units by buyers who differ in a basic taste, network benefits must enter willingness to
pay additively through expected network size (Economides 1996, p. 681; Jamison's description of "linear demand
functions similar to those of Katz and Shapiro"), and the analysis is a single period.

**Independent and dependent variables.** In model terms, the exogenous objects are the number of firms, costs, the
network-benefit function and the compatibility regime; the endogenous objects are firms' outputs, prices, industry
output, profits and consumer surplus. The comparative statics take the compatibility regime and firms' expected
network sizes or reputations as the independent variables.

**Statistical methods.** Does not apply. The "methods" are equilibrium derivations and comparisons of equilibria.

**Reported effect sizes.** None in the statistical sense. The results are signed comparisons, listed in the table
below.

**Confidence intervals.** Do not apply.

**p-values.** Do not apply.

**Missing data treatment.** Does not apply.

**Stated limitations.** From the secondary accounts, the analysis is static, so it cannot speak to the timing of
adoption, which the authors took up in later papers (Katz and Shapiro 1986, 1992, as cited in the 1994 survey and
in Economides 1996). Equilibria need not be unique, because expectations can coordinate on different
outcomes (the 1994 survey, p. 106, cites the 1985 paper for "multiple corner
equilibria in which a single system dominates").

## Interpretation notes

- The headline results are signed comparisons inside a specific model. They follow from fulfilled expectations,
  quantity competition and network benefits that enter willingness to pay additively, and they should be read as
  conditional on those choices.
- The size-and-compatibility result is a statement about bias, meaning the direction of the gap between private
  and social incentives. It is often paraphrased as "large firms oppose compatibility," which is stronger. A large
  firm can still choose compatibility when its private gain is positive, for instance when it can charge rivals'
  customers for access. The authors' 1994 survey (p. 111) adds that side payments make it more likely that firms
  adopt the regime that maximizes industry profits.
- The model's network is the set of users of compatible products. In the charging application in this repository,
  the network is a set of chargers that is a complement to the car, which corresponds to the paper's indirect or
  "virtual network" case rather than the telephone case.
- In this repository's rerun, the paper's prediction that a brand's sales rise with its own network's size under
  incompatibility does not appear at the level of Washington ZIP codes, and the largest charging network opened most
  of its stations. That is a test of the model's predictions on one market, not a reproduction of the paper, which
  contains no data.

## Unanswered questions

- Whether the 1985 paper's results hold under price competition rather than quantity competition, and with
  differentiated products. Later work cited in the 1994 survey takes this up; I could not confirm what the 1985
  paper says about it.
- How sensitive the size-and-compatibility result is to the shape of the network-benefit function and to how
  expectations are formed when several equilibria exist.
- Whether the welfare comparisons account for the fixed costs of compatibility in the same units as output gains,
  and how adapter costs are treated.
- How the conclusions change when the large firm can price access for rivals' customers, which is the situation
  observed in the charging market today.
